## 1. APP CONCEPT & VISION

Selects is a film discovery app that learns your taste and finds your next film in under five minutes. It solves decision fatigue and broken recommendation engines — the 40-minute browse that ends in a compromise.

**Core thesis:** Block out the noise. Surface the best film recommendation with minimum friction. The algorithm wasn't built for you — it was built to keep you watching. Those are not the same thing.

**The Spotify comp:** "It's like Spotify for films — except it actually knows what I'm in the mood for." — Early beta user, 2025

---

## 2. TARGET AUDIENCE

**Primary — The Curious Casual:**
People who care deeply about what they watch, feel overwhelmed by streaming choice, and are underserved by algorithms. They don't have film vocabulary yet but know when something hits.

**Secondary — The Film Obsessive:**
YouTube film reviewers, Letterboxd power users, collector culture (Funko pops, PlayStation collectors, figure collectors), the ones who stay through the credits, people who have opinions about cinematographers.

**Emotional state:** Frustrated → designing to make them Curious.

**Tech profile:** Mobile-native. Mid-20s to late-30s. Tech-comfortable but culture-first.

## 3. FEATURE SET & NAMES

| Feature | Name | What It Does | Say It Naturally |
|---|---|---|---|
| Swipe discovery | **Orbit** | Maps cinema to instinct across 4 directions: mood, story, theme, visual | "run an Orbit" |
| Hidden connections AI | **The Pull** | Passive AI finds hidden connective tissue across films you browse | "something's pulling" |
| Live sentiment | **The Word** | Live Reddit/social sentiment before you commit | "check The Word" |

**Orbit directions:**
- ↑ Up — Mood match / emotional resonance
- ← Left — Storytelling / narrative structure
- ↓ Down — Thematic / ideas and meaning
- → Right — Visual / cinematography and style

**Onboarding questions:**
1. Film you'd watch forever in a private theater (positive anchor)
2. Film everyone loved but you didn't (negative anchor)
3. Preference: Screenplay / Cast / Action / Experience

**Import paths:** Letterboxd CSV, IMDB profile, streaming history, Younify Connect SDK

## 4. BRAND VOICE & IDENTITY

### The One-Line Brief
Selects speaks like a film obsessive who also happens to know exactly what you need tonight — direct, warm, a little confrontational, never trying too hard.

### References
- **Reigns NYC** — collector culture aesthetic, tactical, self-aware
- **A24** — editorial restraint with emotional gut-punches
- **Off-White / Virgil Abloh** — bold typography, quotation marks as visual language, typographically confident
- **Greenlights / McConaughey** — raw, journaled, stream-of-consciousness, confident without being polished

### Voice Pillars

**1. Declarative, not descriptive**
Never explain what Selects does. State what it means.
❌ *Selects uses AI to analyze your viewing history and surface personalized recommendations.*
✅ *It knows you. Finally.*

**2. Short sentences that land like punches**
One thought. Full stop. Move on.
❌ *Finding a great film to watch tonight shouldn't take as long as watching the film itself.*
✅ *Finding a film shouldn't take longer than watching one.*

**3. The quotation mark move — borrowed from Off-White**
Take a common phrase, put it in quotes, make it strange and specific.
> Your next film. "Recommended."
> Tonight's pick. "Personalised."
> The algorithm. "For you."

**4. Speak to the frustration first, then flip to curiosity**
Always enter from the frustrated state, then open a door.
> You've been here before. Forty minutes. Nothing. Again.
> That ends tonight.

**5. Anti-establishment without being angry**
Selects doesn't attack Netflix. It simply exists somewhere better. Confident distance, not rage.
❌ *Netflix only recommends what makes them money.*
✅ *Selects has no agenda. Just your taste.*

**6. The one, not the many**
Every line of copy should make the reader feel like Selects was built specifically for them.
❌ *Personalized recommendations for film lovers.*
✅ *Built for the one. Not the many.*

### Vocabulary

**Words Selects uses:**
taste · instinct · feel · DNA · signal · constellation · depth · discover · curated · earned · honest · yours · direct · sharp · quiet · personal · rabbit hole · five minutes · tonight · the one · blocks away · noise · yours alone

**Words Selects NEVER uses:**
smart · powerful · seamless · intuitive · robust · leverage · solution · platform · users · consumers · content · algorithm (except as villain) · experience (as noun) · journey · for everyone

### The Brand Personality
Selects is the friend who watched 300 films last year, exists completely outside the mainstream recommendation machine, and always knows exactly what you need tonight without you having to justify your taste. It doesn't compete with Netflix. It's not in the same neighborhood.

## 9. COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE

| Competitor | What They Do | The Gap |
|---|---|---|
| Netflix / Prime | Recommends what's profitable for the platform | Content-agnostic. No taste engine. |
| IMDb / RT | Aggregates strangers' opinions | Not personalized. Noise. |
| Letterboxd | Logs history beautifully | Zero recommendation AI. |
| Queue | Tinder swipe mechanic, Match Group DNA | Solves "what to watch broadly." We solve taste identity. |
| JustWatch | Aggregates where to stream | No taste, pure navigation. Built $1.5B on affiliate clicks alone. |
| Filmatic | Small AI recommendation app | Mediocre execution. |

**Primary threat:** Queue — Match Group founders, Tinder swipe methodology, well-funded.
**Counter:** They solve "what to watch broadly." Selects solves taste identity — which is deeper and stickier. Orbit isn't just swipe-to-discover, it's swipe-to-reveal-yourself.

**Platform risk, not competitors:** Netflix/Prime will always prioritize their own content. Selects is content-agnostic — a feature, not a bug.

## 18. LINKEDIN ARTICLE V1 (ORIGINAL)

# The Streaming Industry Gave You Everything. Except a Way to Find It.

Last Friday I spent 40 minutes looking for a film to watch.

I have four streaming subscriptions. Combined, that's somewhere north of 10,000 titles. I scrolled through Netflix. I checked Prime. I opened Letterboxd to see what my friends had watched. I Googled "best films 2024." I read three "hidden gems" listicles that all recommended the same twelve movies.

I ended up rewatching something I'd already seen.

That's not a me problem. I watch over 300 films a year. I have opinions about cinematographers. I stay through the credits. If anyone should be able to find a film, it's me.

And I still couldn't.

---

### The numbers are worse than you think

I assumed this was a personal quirk. It isn't.

Americans now spend an average of 110 hours per year — nearly five full days — just deciding what to watch. That's according to a 2024 UserTesting survey of 4,000 adults across the US, UK, and Australia. Sixteen minutes every single day, gone. Not watching. Deciding.

41% of streamers say they've canceled a subscription specifically because of decision fatigue, according to CivicScience data from late 2025. Nearly half of all subscribers — 47%, per Deloitte's latest Digital Media Trends report — say they're paying too much for what they're getting. Streaming spending dropped 23% in 2024. People aren't falling out of love with film. They're falling out of love with the experience of trying to find it.

We built the most abundant film library in human history. Then we made it nearly impossible to navigate.

---

### Why the algorithm isn't the answer

Here's the part nobody says out loud: the recommendation algorithm was never built for you.

It was built for retention. For watch time. For engagement metrics that justify the next earnings call. Netflix doesn't recommend the film that's right for you — it recommends the film that keeps you on Netflix longest. Those are not the same thing.

IMDb aggregates the opinions of strangers. Rotten Tomatoes tells you what critics thought on opening weekend. Letterboxd — which I love, and which has grown to 17 million users in four years — tells you what your friends watched. None of these tools know what you want tonight. None of them have ever tried to find out.

The result is a strange paradox: we have more access to great cinema than any generation in history, and less ability to find it than ever. The problem isn't the library. It's that nobody built a guide.

---

### What I think is actually broken

I've been thinking about this for a long time, and I think the industry has misdiagnosed the problem.

The assumption is that people can't find films because there are too many choices. So the answer must be fewer choices — algorithms that narrow the library down to a manageable list.

But that's not what's actually happening when you spend 40 minutes browsing. You're not overwhelmed by abundance. You're underwhelmed by relevance. Everything being offered feels like it was made for someone else.

The real problem is that nobody has ever built a tool that understands how you experience film. Not what you've watched — why it stayed with you. Not your genre preferences — your taste. The unnamed feeling that connects the last five films you loved. The thread between them that you've never articulated but would recognize immediately if someone named it.

Taste is not the same as history. Taste is not the same as preference. Taste is something deeper, more personal, and almost impossible to express in a dropdown menu. Every recommendation engine in existence asks you to express it in a dropdown menu.

---

### The discovery maturity model nobody talks about

If you pay attention to how people actually look for films, there are roughly five tiers:

**Tier 1:** Title, actor, or director search. You already know what you want.
**Tier 2:** Genre combinations. Crime thriller. Horror comedy. You have a shape in mind.
**Tier 3:** Themes and vibes. "Something that feels like a Sunday afternoon." You're searching for a sensation.
**Tier 4:** Trusted word of mouth. Someone who knows you tells you to watch something.
**Tier 5:** Rabbit hole discovery. You follow a director to their collaborators. The film becomes the reward.

Every streaming platform is built for tiers 1 and 2. The algorithms are built for tier 2, on a good day. Tiers 3, 4, and 5 — where the best discoveries actually happen — are completely unserved by any product that exists today.

That's the gap. That's the problem nobody has named clearly until now.

## 19. LINKEDIN ARTICLE V2 (FINAL — USE THIS ONE)

# You Have Access to 50,000 Films. You Still Can't Find One to Watch Tonight.

Last Friday I spent 40 minutes looking for a film.

Four streaming subscriptions. Thousands of titles. I scrolled, Googled "best films 2024," read three listicles that all recommended the same twelve movies, and ended up rewatching something I'd already seen.

I don't think that's a me problem. I think that's everyone's problem.

---

Here's the number that stopped me when I found it.

Amazon Prime Video has over 9,000 films. Of those, a significant number are rated below 6.0 on IMDb. MAX — the platform with the highest quality ratio — has 481 films rated over 7.5. Out of thousands.

So for every genuinely great film on the best streaming platform, there are roughly six that aren't.

You're not bad at finding films. You're searching for signal in a library that's mostly noise.

---

And it gets worse.

53% of US households have at least four streaming subscriptions. That's a combined library of somewhere around 50,000 titles. And yet the most-watched film of 2024 — across all of them — was Moana, with 13 billion minutes viewed.

Moana. 2016. In a library of 50,000 films.

We're not overwhelmed by choice. We're defaulting to the familiar because finding something new feels like work.

---

The way we actually look for films has never been solved.

There are roughly five ways people search:

**Tier 1 — Title search.** You already know what you want.
**Tier 2 — Genre.** Crime thriller. Horror comedy.
**Tier 3 — Vibe.** "Something that feels like a rainy Sunday." "Like Eternal Sunshine but darker."
**Tier 4 — Word of mouth.** Someone who knows you tells you to watch it.
**Tier 5 — Rabbit hole.** You follow a director to their collaborators, a cinematographer to their other films. The discovery becomes the reward.

Every streaming platform is built for tiers 1 and 2. Nobody has seriously tried to build for 3, 4, and 5.

That's where the best film experiences actually happen. And there's no tool for it.

---

The algorithm isn't trying to find your film.

It's trying to keep you on the platform. Those are not the same thing.

It doesn't know that you stayed through the credits of a film nobody else saw. It doesn't know what connected two films you've loved for years that you've never been able to explain. It knows what performed. It knows what a committee approved. It knows what kept people watching at 2am.

It doesn't know you.

And that's the actual problem. Not the size of the library. Not the number of subscriptions. The fact that none of these tools were ever built around individual taste — just collective behavior.

---

We've solved this for music. Spotify knows your taste well enough to predict what you'll love before you hear it. Nobody has done that for film yet.

That's a gap that shouldn't exist in 2026.

The 40-minute Friday night browse shouldn't still be a universal experience. The rabbit hole shouldn't be something you have to do manually. Finding the film that was made for you shouldn't feel like work.

We all deserve better than settling.
